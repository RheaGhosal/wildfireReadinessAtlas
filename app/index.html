Why I built this

Wildfire information should be a starting point everyone can use.

While researching wildfire risk in Napa Valley, I kept finding that the best data lived in tools built for experts. I designed and built Wildfire Readiness Atlas to change that: a place where students, families, and communities can explore official risk data, ask better questions about what it leaves out, and start preparing.

I developed the Atlas with Python, GeoPandas, Shapely, and GeoJSON to process FEMA county-level risk and Census boundary data, then built an interactive JavaScript dashboard that connects the results with live NASA FIRMS satellite detections and National Weather Service alerts.

Student field guide

Maps are a starting point, not the answer.

  1. Read critically. What measure is shown? When was it last updated?
  2. Look for gaps. Whose needs—language, disability access, transportation, housing, or air filtration—might not appear on a map?
  3. Find local support. Identify the school, library, clinic, emergency-management office, and trusted people your family can contact.
  4. Prepare before smoke or evacuation. Make a family communication plan and follow local officials during an emergency.

Method and limits

What this dashboard does—and does not—claim

It aggregates county-level FEMA National Risk Index wildfire relative-risk scores into a state-level educational view, weighting each county by FEMA's reported population. This keeps the statewide comparison focused on the people represented by the source data while leaving the county-level map available for local context. It does not predict the next fire, replace local warnings, rank communities by worthiness, or determine where people should live.
